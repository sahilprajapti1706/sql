
## 7. What is normalization and why is it used?

### Answer

**Normalization is the process of organizing data in a relational database to reduce unnecessary data redundancy and prevent data anomalies.**

The basic idea is to **store each piece of information in an appropriate place and connect related data using keys**, rather than repeatedly storing the same information.

For example, instead of storing a customer's details repeatedly in every order:

```text
orders
+----------+------------+---------------+--------+
| order_id | customer   | customer_city | amount |
+----------+------------+---------------+--------+
| 101      | Sahil      | Gorakhpur     | 500    |
| 102      | Sahil      | Gorakhpur     | 800    |
| 103      | Rahul      | Delhi         | 300    |
+----------+------------+---------------+--------+
```

we can separate customers and orders:

```text
customers
+-------------+--------+-----------+
| customer_id | name   | city      |
+-------------+--------+-----------+
| 1           | Sahil  | Gorakhpur |
| 2           | Rahul  | Delhi     |
+-------------+--------+-----------+

orders
+----------+-------------+--------+
| order_id | customer_id | amount |
+----------+-------------+--------+
| 101      | 1           | 500    |
| 102      | 1           | 800    |
| 103      | 2           | 300    |
+----------+-------------+--------+
```

Now `customer_id` acts as the link between the tables.

---

## Why is normalization used?

The main goals are:

### 1. Reduce data redundancy

Without normalization, the same information may be stored repeatedly.

For example:

```text
Sahil | Gorakhpur
Sahil | Gorakhpur
Sahil | Gorakhpur
```

If Sahil changes his city, multiple records may need to be updated.

After normalization:

```text
customers
1 | Sahil | Gorakhpur
```

The information is stored once.

---

### 2. Prevent update anomalies

Suppose Sahil's city changes from Gorakhpur to Lucknow.

If his city is stored in 100 order records, we would need to update all 100 records.

If one record is missed:

```text
Order 101 → Lucknow
Order 102 → Lucknow
Order 103 → Gorakhpur  ← inconsistent
```

Normalization reduces this problem by storing the customer's city in one appropriate location.

---

### 3. Prevent insertion anomalies

Consider a table that stores:

```text
student_id | student_name | course_id | course_name
```

If a new course has been created but **no student has enrolled yet**, we may not be able to store the course information naturally in this table.

Separating entities into appropriate tables solves this:

```text
students
courses
enrollments
```

Now a course can exist independently of whether someone has enrolled in it.

---

### 4. Prevent deletion anomalies

Suppose the last student enrolled in a particular course is deleted.

If course information exists only in the enrollment table, deleting that student's record might also accidentally remove the only stored information about the course.

Normalization separates the entities so that deleting an enrollment does not necessarily delete the course itself.

---

# Normal Forms

Normalization is commonly discussed using **normal forms**.

The most important ones for placement interviews are:

* **1NF — First Normal Form**
* **2NF — Second Normal Form**
* **3NF — Third Normal Form**
* **BCNF — Boyce-Codd Normal Form**

You do not always need to normalize a database all the way to the highest possible normal form. The appropriate design depends on the application's requirements.

---

## 1NF — First Normal Form

A table is generally considered to be in **1NF** when each field contains an atomic/single value rather than a repeating list or group.

Bad design:

```text
student_id | name  | phone_numbers
1          | Sahil | 9876, 9123
```

The `phone_numbers` column contains multiple values.

A normalized design could be:

```text
students
student_id | name
1          | Sahil

student_phones
student_id | phone
1          | 9876
1          | 9123
```

This makes the individual phone values easier to query and manage.

> **Interview note:** "Atomic" should be understood in the context of the database's intended operations; 1NF is not simply a universal rule that every piece of text must be split into the smallest possible characters.

---

## 2NF — Second Normal Form

A table is in **2NF** when it is in 1NF and every non-key attribute depends on the **whole primary key**, not just part of a composite key.

Consider:

```text
enrollments
+------------+-----------+--------------+-------------+
| student_id | course_id | student_name | course_name |
+------------+-----------+--------------+-------------+
```

Suppose:

```text
Primary Key = (student_id, course_id)
```

But:

```text
student_name → depends only on student_id
course_name  → depends only on course_id
```

These attributes do not depend on the **whole composite key**.

We can separate them:

```text
students
student_id | student_name

courses
course_id | course_name

enrollments
student_id | course_id
```

Now each piece of information belongs to the appropriate table.

---

## 3NF — Third Normal Form

A table is in **3NF** when it is in 2NF and non-key attributes do not depend on other non-key attributes.

Consider:

```text
employees
+-------------+-------------+--------------+-------------+
| employee_id | employee_name| department_id| department_name|
+-------------+-------------+--------------+-------------+
```

Suppose:

```text
employee_id → department_id
department_id → department_name
```

Therefore:

```text
employee_id → department_id → department_name
```

`department_name` depends on `department_id`, rather than directly on the employee's key.

We can separate:

```text
employees
employee_id | employee_name | department_id

departments
department_id | department_name
```

Now the department information is stored once.

---

# What problems does normalization solve?

A useful way to remember normalization is:

```text
Normalization
     ↓
Less unnecessary duplication
     ↓
Fewer inconsistencies
     ↓
Fewer insertion/update/deletion anomalies
     ↓
Better data integrity
```

---

# Does normalization always improve performance?

**Not necessarily.**

Normalization can require more tables and therefore more `JOIN`s when retrieving data.

For example:

```text
customers
      ↓
JOIN
      ↓
orders
      ↓
JOIN
      ↓
products
```

For some read-heavy workloads, deliberately storing some duplicated/derived data can reduce expensive joins. This is called **denormalization**.

So database design involves a trade-off:

| Normalization                           | Denormalization                        |
| --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Reduces redundancy                      | Intentionally allows some redundancy   |
| Improves consistency                    | Can improve read performance           |
| More separate tables                    | Fewer joins in some queries            |
| Often easier to maintain data integrity | Can require additional synchronization |
| May require more joins                  | May use more storage                   |

Neither approach is automatically better. The choice depends on the workload, consistency requirements, query patterns, and scale.

## Practical Example

Imagine a marketplace application.

A poorly structured table might contain:

```text
order_id
customer_name
customer_email
customer_city
product_name
product_price
```

If the same customer places 100 orders, their information may be repeated 100 times.

A more normalized design might separate:

```text
customers
orders
products
order_items
```

with relationships such as:

```text
customers
    │
    └── orders
          │
          └── order_items
                 │
                 └── products
```

This makes it easier to maintain customer and product information consistently.

## Common Mistakes

### 1. "Normalization means creating as many tables as possible."

No. The goal is **appropriate organization of data**, not maximizing the number of tables.

### 2. "Normalization always makes queries faster."

Not necessarily. More normalized designs can require additional joins.

### 3. "Normalization means there should never be duplicate values."

No. Normalization primarily reduces **unnecessary redundancy**. Duplicate values can legitimately exist—for example, many orders may have the same `customer_id`.

### 4. "Normalization and indexing are the same thing."

They solve different problems:

* **Normalization** → organizes data and reduces redundancy.
* **Indexing** → improves the efficiency of data retrieval for appropriate query patterns.

---

## Interview Takeaway

> **Normalization is the process of organizing relational data to reduce unnecessary redundancy and prevent insertion, update, and deletion anomalies. It typically involves decomposing large tables into related tables and connecting them using keys.**

For interviews, remember:

```text
1NF → Atomic values / no repeating groups
2NF → No partial dependency on a composite key
3NF → No transitive dependency of non-key attributes
```

And the practical trade-off:

> **Normalization improves data integrity and maintainability, but excessive normalization can increase the number of joins, so real systems sometimes use controlled denormalization for performance.**
